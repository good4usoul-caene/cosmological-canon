In post #1877.5 @Darrell Bender has presented the following example
“Consider two rigid rods, each of length l, with identical clocks moving at the same rate at the ends of each rod. At a certain time t the rods coincide. One rod moves to the left with velocity v; one rod moves to the right with velocity v. At the time t, all clocks are set to zero and a ray of light is emitted to the right from the common left endpoint of the two rods. According to Einstein's special theory of relativity, the velocity of light as measured in each moving system is c; so that when the light ray reaches the right endpoint of the rod moving to the left, the right endpoint of the rod moving to the right must be in the same place. This is clearly impossible. This proves that the velocity of light as measured in the moving systems is not c and that clocks in the moving system are not synchronous.”
=========
Here is a proof that according to SRT the SOL in each rod of this example is c, independently of the value and the sign of the velocity.
------
IF in some IRF endowed with space and time coordinates denoted by x and t, motions of the left and right end points of a rod of length L are given by the world lines
. . . xL(t) = v t . . . xR(t) = L + v t . . |v|<c
And a light pulse motion is given by
. . . xP(t) = c t,
THEN according to SRT
(i) the event of hitting the right end point by the pulse possesses coordinates
. . . t* = L/(c-v) . . . x* = c t* = c L/(c-v)
(ii) The same event in the IRF' comoving with the rod possessed coordinates given by the image of the Lorentz transformation corresponding to velocity v as follows
. . . t*' = γ (t* - v x*/c²) = γ L/c
. . . x*' = γ (x* - v t*) = γ L
(iii) In particular the SOL in the IRF' equals
. . . c' = x*'/t*' = c.

The vertical line from emitter to detector in a container (carriage) represents the presumed 2D state of light with respect to an external observer. That is, the energy from emitter to an observer occurs over the shortest distance of space. But this is merely a geometric way of demonstrating that light within the carriage (which is emitted independent of the train’s motion) is moving away from the external (station) observer.
That's why physicists don't measure observed time dilation with respect to an external observer by discussing the motion of light from, for example, the front to the back of the train. The zig-zag pattern that the external observer allegedly sees has more to do with what would happen in a hypothetical onboard photon clock.
The extra time that energy (light) needs to reach the detector’s new position when the carriage is moving “forward” (or any other direction), can be represented as a diagonal vector that forms the basis for the geometric illustration of gamma (as Feynman specifically notes).
Note that when atoms are moving faster, “the external observer sees the atom’s “clock” running slow, so photons are emitted less frequently in [according to his] time.”
However, “In the atom’s own rest frame, electrons emit photons at their normal rate. The quantum transitions (like an electron dropping from one energy level to another) happen with the same probabilities and lifetimes as if the atom were stationary.”
But the atom’s “knowledge” of how fast it is emitting photos would be determined by observing its own clock (much as the passenger of a carriage oberves her clock), which itself would be slowing down for the same reasons to a proportionate extent.
This suggests that the observer observes two types of interrelated time dilation (observational and physical):
A. Photons are emitted at a slower rate when the atom is moving at higher velocities. This would result in observational red shifting with regards to external observer observing departing planes.
B. The distance between energy (light) pulses is greater when an object made of atoms recedes at higher velocities. This would result in permanent physical time dilation, e.g., slower clocks on moving planes.
Presumably, these two types of red-shifting would somewhat correspond to each other:
That’s because it takes a longer time (over a longer distance), according to an external observer, for:
A. Physical time dilation: The moving atom emits a photon less frequnelty as a result of the greater distance the atom is traveling through space. (Emission is based on the time between energy transitions within the atom from one atomic level to another so that a photon is emitted).
B. Observational time dilation: The propagation of energy (as light) from the atom to the external observer via the emitted photons (as observed by passenger vs external observer).
Note that in practice, the direction in which light is emitted within the container is irrelevant, since the question is not really how far light travels from one side to another within the container, but rather how much slower light is traveling through space (the “vacuum”) with regards to the rate of photon of emission from the atom, and the rate at which the light is receding from the external observer.
The station observer is NOT counting, timing, or measuring the distance that light beams travel within the carriage!!
That is, in terms of observational time dilation, the “ticks” (in question with regards to time dilation) are not actually counted in terms of light reaching a given wall within the train, but by the ticks on the external observer’s clock. Such ticks reflect the rate at which the external observer observes pulses from the light within container (carriage) and within the carriage’s clock to occur (as they are red-shifted). Rather he is simply measuring how long it takes for (any) light emitted from the carriage to reach him at the station.
The greater the carriage’s acceleration, the greater the observed time dilation, since “the relativistic Doppler formula, which grows very steeply as velocity approaches the speed of light. “

The Stoic, Zeno of Citium, used a simple hand metaphor to distinguish between raw impressions, agreement, belief, and knowledge. An open palm represented the impression itself — something that arrives whether we agree or not. Closing the hand showed assent — the act of choosing to agree. A fist symbolized belief — conviction that holds fast. Enclosing the fist with the other hand represented knowledge — secure, unshakable grasp. The point wasn’t about mathematics or proving a theorem; it was about showing that human judgment is active: impressions strike us, but assent is voluntary. Philosophy here is about the discipline of assent — deciding whether to agree, suspend, or reject.
Ancient sources like Cicero, Sextus Empiricus, and Diogenes Laertius preserve Zeno’s fist metaphor, but they don’t mention a gesture for rejection. The focus was on illustrating the positive progression toward knowledge, not the negative act of denial. We could extend Zeno’s hand‑metaphor with the palm turned outward (a stop gesture): “I hear the impression, but I reject it.”

In the case of John 3, when the Son of Man says to Nicodemus, “You are Israel’s teacher and you do not know this?” I see that the Son of Man makes an informal fallacy if his desire is truly to teach Nicodemus (palm turned outward).
