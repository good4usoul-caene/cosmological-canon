## Part 1: The Pursuit and the Discovery (6 CE)

A week after Herod's wedding, Herodias and he accompanied a group of Romans in pursuit of Joseph and Mary, who had fled Cana with the child Jesusa Bara Bas.  Since the trail had gone cold, it occurred to them to ransack their house in Nazareth.

Herodias stepped inside, her eyes adjusting to the dim light. The house was sparse, but in the corner stood an *aron* (a large wooden chest for scrolls).

She opened the lid, and within were dozens of leather-wrapped scrolls.  She ran her fingers over the leather, and read the titles.

Leviticus, Numbers, Job, Zechariah, Ruth, Ezekiel, ...  One caught her eye, because it was wrapped more like a gift...  "Isaiah"  She lifted up it's leather-bound case, and read the inscription.  

"Mary, daughter of Joachim.  Born 3740 Anno Mundi."  Herodias counted... 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40.  Year 7 Augustus.  Mary was seven years older than her.  In another hand, was written, "Jesus, son of Joseph and Mary.  Born 3754 Anno Mundi."  Jesus was born of Mary at age 14.  Jesus was seven years younger.  She was right in between. 

One of Herod's men entered, having interrogated many of the people in town.  He seemed aimed recklessly to rummage through the scrolls in the aron, but Herodias warned him:  "Be very, very careful with those.  They're worth more than you can afford."  He stopped and gazed into the box.

Herodias ran her fingers over the coverings, then grasped the *etz chaim* (the wooden rods attached to the scroll) to unroll Isaiah carefully across the table. 

Behind her, Herod asked the investigator "Have you learned anything, talking to the people?"  

The investigator replied, "They say that Jesusa Bara Bas is not from here; never heard of her.  Though they did mention Jesus.  Some of them call him Immanuel."  He chuckles.  "They say Mary was a virgin when she had him."

At that moment the investigator said "Immanuel", Herodias was reading Immanuel's name in the scroll.  She was open to this sort of coincidence, reckoning that the divine was making contact.  The scroll explained that Immanuel means "God with us".  Quite an honorific for such a young man.  She continued to read until she came to the next chapter.  She unrolled Isaiah 8 and read aloud.

> "The Lord said to me, 'Take a large scroll and write on it with an ordinary pen:  Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz.' Then I made love to the prophetess, and she conceived and gave birth to a son."

Herodias looked up, her heart pounding, imagining herself as the prophetess. "Made love?", she wondered.  She thought about the brief rutting Herod had called "consummation" the night before, and tried to decide whether that counted.

She whispered to herself, "but, I am the prophetess."  (ἀλλὰ ἐγώ εἰμι ἡ προφήτις)

## Part 2: Gabriel's Selection (~10 CE)

In the next few years, Herod and the counsel kept a close eye on Jesus' ministry.  He was extremely popular, but nothing he said threatened the authority of Rome.  His teaching on taxation was "Give to Caesar what is Caesar's, and give to God what is God's," which suited the tax collectors perfectly well.  Moreover, Herodias warned Herod to leave him be.  

Meanwhile, John's main focus seemed quite constructive; the baptisms in the Jordan were often discussed in the same breath with people's appreciation of the Roman bath-houses.  His vocal criticisms were usually aimed toward Jewish religious leaders; judges and lawyers, rather than Roman ones.

Four years had passed since the wedding at Cana.   Four years of hope, of prayers whispered in the dark, of Herodias' monthly disappointments.  Four years of whispers in the court:   "Perhaps Herod is impotent.   Perhaps the marriage is cursed."

Herod himself began to believe it.  He had fathered children before—he was certain of it.  But with Herodias, nothing took root. 

One evening, Quirinius arrived at Herod's palace in Tiberias.  He carried no scrolls, no census reports.   He came alone, and he came with a proposal.

"My lord," Quirinius said, "I have been considering your...  situation."

Herod glanced up from his wine.   "My situation?"

"The lack of an heir," Quirinius said bluntly.  "You've been married four years.  Herodias has not conceived.  The people are beginning to talk."

Herod's jaw tightened.  "What do you propose?"

Quirinius leaned forward.  "You remember what I told you at Cana?  About rewriting the census?  About making your child the one born under the sign?"

Herod nodded.

"But you need a child first," Quirinius said.  "And if you cannot father one..."

"Then what?" Herod snapped. 

Quirinius did not flinch.  "Then we find someone who can."

---

Herod stared at him for a long moment.  Then he laughed—a bitter, hollow sound.  "You're suggesting I let another man bed my wife."

"I'm suggesting," Quirinius said carefully, "that we fulfill the prophecy.  Isaiah 8:3 says, 'I made love to the prophetess, and she conceived.'  Herodias is the prophetess.  But the prophecy does not say *you* must be the one who fulfills it."

Herod's laughter died.   He set down his cup. 

"You want me to... find a surrogate?"

"I want you to find a father for your heir," Quirinius said.  "Someone strong.   Someone loyal.  Someone who will never speak of this."

Herod closed his eyes.  He thought of the census records, already destroyed.  He thought of the triple conjunction, erased from history.  He thought of Salome—the daughter he did not yet have, the child who would claim the prophecy.

"How would we even do this?" Herod asked quietly. 

Quirinius smiled.  "I have already begun.  I've gathered one hundred men—Praetorians, the finest soldiers of Rome.  Each one has fathered children.  Each one is loyal to your dynasty.  You will interview them.   Herodias will choose."

---

The interviews took place over three weeks.  Herod set up a small chamber in the palace, away from prying eyes.  Quirinius brought the men in one by one. 

Herod asked each the same questions: 

"How many children do you have?"

"Are you loyal to Rome?"

"Can you keep a secret?"

Some men were dismissed immediately—too old, too talkative, too uncertain.  Others passed Herod's tests but failed to impress him.  By the end of the second week, only seven remained.

Herod summoned Herodias.

"These seven," he said, gesturing to the men lined up in the courtyard, "are fit for the task.  The choice is yours."

Herodias walked among them, studying each face.  Some were tall, some broad-shouldered.  Some had kind eyes; others, hard. 

She stopped before a man with dark eyes and a quiet demeanor.  He stood at attention, his posture perfect, his gaze steady.

"What is your name?" she asked.

"Gabriel, my lady."

Herodias smiled faintly.  "Gabriel.  'God is my strength.'  You will do."

---

Gabriel was stationed in Nazareth as one of Herodias' bodyguards.  Publicly, he was simply another soldier.  Privately, Herod and Herodias and Quirinius knew his true purpose.

But they would wait.  They would wait for the right moment—the moment when Augustus died, when Tiberius ascended, when the stars aligned.

Only then would Gabriel fulfill his role.

Only then would the child be conceived.  She hoped for a boy.  She wouldn't call him "Maher Shalal Hash Baz" but she would have Gabriel write it on the scroll, on the night of the conception.

## Part 3: Isaiah 8 Obsession (10–14 CE)

Herodias moved into Mary's former house in Nazareth, close to where Herod was building his new capital, Tiberias. She spent her days reading the Torah, practicing divination, and preparing for the child she would bear. 

One evening, Herod visited her.  "You spend all your time with those scrolls," he said. "What is it you seek?"

"Prophecy," Herodias said. "Isaiah 8:4 says, 'Before the boy knows how to say "My father" or "My mother," the wealth of Damascus and the plunder of Samaria will be carried off by the king of Assyria.' You, Herod, will be that king."

Herod's eyes gleamed. "And the child?"

"The child," Herodias said, "will be Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz.  'Swift is the booty, speedy is the prey.' The one who brings conquest."

Herod nodded slowly. "Then we wait."

"We wait," Herodias agreed, "for the death of Augustus."

---

### Herodias's Reading of Isaiah 8

Herodias returned to the Torah night after night, tracing the words with her finger. She had become obsessed with Isaiah 8, convinced that it held the key to her destiny. 

**Isaiah 8:3** — "I made love to the prophetess, and she conceived and gave birth to a son."

She whispered to herself, "I am the prophetess. Gabriel is the one who will fulfill Isaiah's role. He will make love to me, and I will conceive."

**Isaiah 8:17** — "I will wait for the Lord, who is hiding his face from the descendants of Jacob."

"The Lord is hiding his face," she said aloud. "He will reveal himself through my child.  Our Solomon will be the one who breaks the covenant with Jacob's line. He will be a new beginning."

**Isaiah 8:19** — "When someone tells you to consult mediums and spiritists, who whisper and mutter, should not a people inquire of their God?"

Herodias smiled faintly. "I have consulted mediums.  I have read bones, listened to birds, watched the stars. But once Solomon is born, I will stop.  He will be the living oracle.  He will replace necromancy with living prophecy.  He will be my King Solomon.  He will be my King Oedipus."

---

### Herod's Reading of Isaiah 8

Herod visited Herodias again a few weeks later. "You've convinced me," he said. "Tell me again what Isaiah says."

Herodias opened the Torah and read aloud: 

**Isaiah 8:1** — "Take a large scroll and write on it with an ordinary pen:  Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz."

Herod nodded. "Gabriel will write it. On the night of the conception."

**Isaiah 8:4** — "Before the boy knows how to say 'My father' or 'My mother,' the wealth of Damascus and the plunder of Samaria will be carried off by the king of Assyria."

Herod's eyes gleamed. "I will be that king.  I will carry off the wealth of Damascus and the plunder of Samaria.  Solomon will be the prophetic child who brings conquest."

Herodias looked at him.  "But the prophecy says 'before the boy knows how to say "My father" or "My mother."' What if we have a daughter?"

Herod waved a hand dismissively. "Then we'll adjust the prophecy. The important thing is that the child is born under the sign.  Quirinius will rewrite the records to make it so."

---

### The Wait (10–14 CE)

For the next four years, Herodias and Herod waited. They waited for Augustus to die.  They waited for the stars to align. They waited for the right moment.

Gabriel remained stationed in Nazareth, a silent presence in Herodias's household. He was patient, disciplined, and loyal. He did not speak of his role, and Herodias did not press him.  They both knew what was expected. 

Herod visited occasionally, always asking the same question: "Has Augustus died yet?"

"Not yet," Herodias would say. "But soon."

"Then we wait," Herod would reply. 

And so they waited.

---

### The Death of Augustus (14 CE)

In the 41st year of Augustus's reign, word came that the emperor had died. Tiberius had ascended to the throne. 

Herodias felt a rush of exhilaration. "It's time," she said to Gabriel. 

Gabriel nodded. "I will write it on the scroll tonight."

That night, Gabriel took a large scroll and an ordinary pen. He wrote in Hebrew: 

**מַהֵר שָׁלָל חָשׁ בַּז**  
*Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz*  
"Swift is the booty, speedy is the prey."

Then he went to Herodias. 

---

### The Conception

Herodias had prepared herself for this moment. She had fasted, prayed, and read the prophecies until she could recite them from memory. 

When Gabriel came to her, she whispered, "I am the prophetess. You are channeling Isaiah: Yahweh is Salvation.  Let it be done according to the word."

The word was not made by vocal chords.  His years of service to Herodias had been done with agape love.  And his expression of love now, was more than words.

Afterward, Herodias held the scroll in her hands and wept. "It is done," she whispered. "The child will be born under the sign. The child will be Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz."

## Part 4: Birth of Salome (14 CE)

So, in the 41st year of Augustus's reign, word had came that the emperor had died. Tiberius had ascended to the throne. Salome was conceived, and it became reigning year 1 of Tiberius.

Nine months passed. Herodias's belly grew.  Herod visited often, pacing nervously. 

"What if it's a girl?" he asked one day.

"Then she will be Salome," Herodias said calmly. "And we will adjust the prophecy."

"But the prophecy says 'the boy,'" Herod protested. 

Herodias smiled faintly. "Prophecy is flexible, Herod. It bends to those who have the courage to shape it."

Herod sighed. "I hope you're right."

"I am," Herodias said. "Trust me."

---

When the child was born, Herod held her in his arms and wept. "A daughter," he said softly. "Not a son."

"Her name is Salome," Herodias said. "And she is the fulfillment of prophecy."

Herod looked down at the infant, swaddled in linen, her tiny fists clenched. "But the prophecy says Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz.  A son."

Herodias took the child from his arms. "Then we will call her Solomon.  Our King Solomon.  Our King Oedipus.  She will be the one who breaks the covenant with Jacob's line.  She will be a new beginning."

Herod frowned. "Solomon is a boy's name."

"And Salome is a girl's," Herodias said. "But prophecy is not bound by names. It is bound by signs.  And this child was born under the sign."

---

Quirinius arrived a week later with scribes and paper. "My lord, it is done.  The records have been rewritten.  The year of Salome's birth is now Year 1 of Tiberias. All trace of the triple conjunction has been erased."

Herod smiled. "Then history will remember my daughter as the child born under the sign."

Quirinius bowed. "It is as you commanded."

---

Herodias held Salome in her arms, gazing out the window at the hills of Nazareth. She whispered to the child: 

> "You are the prophetess.  You are the one who will replace necromancy with living prophecy. You are the living oracle.  And I will no longer consult the dead, for you are here."

Salome, eyes still closed, made no sound.

---

That night, Herodias took out the scroll Gabriel had written on the night of her conception. She unrolled it carefully and read the Hebrew letters:

**מַהֵר שָׁלָל חָשׁ בַּז**  
*Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz*  
"Swift is the booty, speedy is the prey."

She traced the letters with her finger, then looked down at Salome, asleep in her cradle.

"You are not a boy," Herodias whispered. "But you are the prophetic child. You are the one who brings conquest.  You are the one who will carry off the wealth of Damascus and the plunder of Samaria."

She paused, then added softly:

"And you will do it not with strength, but with your voice. Not with a sword, but with your words. Not with a crown, but with your dance."

---

Herod visited the next morning. "I've been thinking," he said. "If Salome is to be the prophetic child, she must be educated. Not just in Torah, but in the ways of empire."

Herodias nodded. "She will learn Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Aramaic. She will learn music, dance, rhetoric, and philosophy. She will be the wisest woman in Galilee."

"And she will be beautiful," Herod added. "More beautiful than her mother."

Herodias smiled faintly. "Flattery, Herod?"

"No," Herod said.  "Truth."

---

That evening, Herodias sat with Gabriel in the courtyard.  Salome was asleep in her cradle, and the stars were beginning to appear overhead.

"You did well," Herodias said quietly.

Gabriel nodded. "She is beautiful."

"She is ours," Herodias said.  "Yours and mine. But the world will call her Herod's."

Gabriel looked at her. "Does that bother you?"

"No," Herodias said. "Prophecy requires sacrifice. And this is mine."

Gabriel reached over and took her hand. "And what of her future?"

Herodias looked up at the stars. "She will dance for kings. She will speak for prophets. And one day, she will ask for something the world cannot refuse."

"What will she ask for?" Gabriel asked.

Herodias smiled. "I don't know yet. But I will teach her to ask with confidence."

---

The years passed. Salome grew strong and beautiful, her laughter filling the halls of Herod's palace. She learned to read and write, to sing and dance, to recite scripture and philosophy. 

Herod doted on her, calling her "my little prophetess." Herodias taught her divination, showing her how to read bones and listen to birds.

And Gabriel watched from a distance, always present but never claiming her as his own.

---

When Salome was five, Herodias took her to the window and pointed to the hills beyond Nazareth. 

"Do you see those hills?" Herodias asked.

Salome nodded.

"One day," Herodias said, "you will stand on those hills and proclaim the prophecy. You will be the one who fulfills Isaiah 8.  You will be Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz."

"But I'm a girl," Salome said. 

"Yes," Herodias said. "And that is your strength. The prophecy does not require a boy. It requires a voice. And you, my daughter, have the loudest voice of all."

---

When Salome was twelve, Herodias sat her down and said: 

"You are no longer a child. You are a woman. And women must be wise, not just beautiful. They must be strong, not just graceful. They must be cunning, not just obedient."

"Why?" Salome asked.

"Because," Herodias said, "the world will try to take everything from you. Your voice.  Your body. Your choice. But if you are wise, strong, and cunning, you can take back what is yours."

"What is mine?" Salome asked. 

Herodias smiled. "Everything."

---

## Part 5: Salome's Early Life (14–26 CE)

Salome grew up in Nazareth, surrounded by stories of her mother's devotion and her father's ambition. She learned to read and write in Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Aramaic.   She studied music, dance, rhetoric, and philosophy. 

Herod doted on her, calling her "my little prophetess." Herodias taught her divination, showing her how to read bones and listen to birds. And Gabriel watched from a distance, always present but never claiming her as his own.

---

### John's Ministry (14–24 CE) - Imprisonment (24-26 CE)

While Salome grew, John the Baptist traveled the countryside, baptizing in the Jordan and building outhouses along the pilgrimage routes. 

"Everyone who is able," John would preach, "needs to make a pilgrimage to the outhouse as needed.   Those who cannot or will not make the trip will find...   well, let's just say, you reap what you sow."

People laughed, but they also listened. And they remembered.

John's reputation grew. He baptized tax collectors, soldiers, and common folk. He preached repentance and hygiene. He built outhouses at crossroads and near wells.  

"I baptize you with water," John said.   "But after me comes one who is more powerful than I, whose sandals I am not worthy to carry. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire."

---

### Jesus of Nazareth's Early Preaching (14–26 CE)

Jesus of Nazareth, meanwhile, began his own ministry.  He taught in synagogues, healed the sick, and cast out demons. He spoke in parables, using stories of farmers, seeds, and harvests to explain the Kingdom of God.

He said:  

"The Kingdom of God is like a mustard seed, which is the smallest of all seeds on earth. Yet when planted, it grows and becomes the largest of all garden plants, with such big branches that the birds can perch in its shade."

"The Kingdom of God is like yeast that a woman took and mixed into about sixty pounds of flour until it worked all through the dough."

"The Kingdom of God is like a treasure hidden in a field. When a man found it, he hid it again, and then in his joy went and sold all he had and bought that field."

His teachings drew crowds.   He never performed a single miracle but he witnessed many, as those who so much as touched the fabric of his robe were healed.  He always sensed it, though, and he turned to them and exclaimed "You have great faith!"  

When others asked for a sign, he said, "God offers only that which is needed.   For this adulterous and sign seeking generation, only one will be given, and that is the Sign of Jonah."

"But which sign of Jonah?" people asked.  

"Which sign do you need?"  Jesus hoped they would ask that the Ninevites repented, and God would relent from their destruction.  Or maybe that Jonah might pull the worm out of the tree that gave him shade. 

But of course, they did not.   They all wanted to see Jonah swallowed by the whale.  So Jesus thought of Cronus and Gaia; how Cronus swallowed the Titans, and which Titan ought return.   "So be it.   For this wicked and adulterous generation, the Son of Man will be buried in the heart of the earth for three days and rise again."

His miracles amazed the people.  But he also drew the attention of the Pharisees, who questioned his authority and challenged his interpretations of the Law. 

"By what authority are you doing these things?" they asked him.  "And who gave you authority to do this?"

Jesus replied, "I will ask you one question.  Answer me, and I will tell you by what authority I am doing these things.  John's baptism—was it from heaven, or of human origin?   Tell me!"

They discussed it among themselves and said, "If we say, 'From heaven,' he will ask, 'Then why didn't you believe him?' But if we say, 'Of human origin'..." (They feared the people, for everyone held that John really was a prophet.)

So they answered Jesus, "We don't know."

Jesus sighed, "Surely the first house of worship for humanity is the bath house.   A blessed sanctuary, and a guide for all people."  Jesus said, "Neither will I tell you by what authority I am doing these things."

### Salome's Education and the Discovery of Sychar (~14–20 CE)

As Salome grew, Herod and Herodias hired tutors from far-reaching places to educate her in Greek, Latin, Hebrew, Aramaic, music, dance, rhetoric, and philosophy.  Among them was a teacher from the far east, a scholar who had traveled from India to teach the wisdom of the Vedas and the epics of the Mahabharata.

One evening, the tutor sat with Salome in the courtyard, recounting the story of Draupadi, the princess born from fire, who married five brothers and bore five sons. He spoke of her beauty, her strength, and her rage when she was humiliated by Dushasana before the court of kings.  

Salome listened, enthralled.   "Did she ever find justice?" she asked.

The tutor smiled faintly. "In the end, her sons were slaughtered, and she ascended to heaven with her husbands. But some believe her spirit was reborn, not in India, but in a small village in Samaria called Sychar."

Herod, who had been listening from the doorway, stepped forward. "Sychar? The village near Jacob's Well?"

The tutor nodded. "Yes, my lord. It is said that a woman there, called Draupi, bears the same name and the same fire.   She has five sons, just as the Draupadi of old, but she also had a daughter."

Herod turned to Herodias, who had been watching silently. "Fancy that.  Reincarnation, right here under our noses!"    

"Had a daughter?" Herodias asks.

"She left for Egypt years ago.  She was called Bara Bas.  Little has been heard from her."

Herod paused, stunned.  But Herodias didn't miss a beat.  "Reincarnation...  For what reason are souls reincarnated?  Is it for punishment, or to complete an unfulfilled task?"

The tutor says, "Sometimes one, and sometimes both.  We believe that once a soul has accomplished all tasks that have been set out by the creator, they no longer need be reincarnated, and they can go to a better place."

"So, then, what task was left incomplete by Draupadi and her sons?"

"For the sons, time is incomplete.  In the Bhagavad Gita, Time says it will never be through with them.  But for the mother, Draupadi, her task was to fully restore her father Drupada's kingdom."

Herodias nodded slowly, then took Herod aside. 

Herod said, "We should capture this Draupi woman, and hold her for ransom.  Then Bara will surely return."

Herodias said.  "That's an option, but let's not act prematurely.  Send scouts to Sychar.  Let them observe, but do not act yet. We must understand the full scope of what we are dealing with.  And in the meantime, have our scholars study the Mahabharata."

## Part 6: Herod's Encounter with John (~22–26 CE)

By the year 22 CE, John the Baptist had built a reputation across Galilee and Judea.    He baptized in the Jordan, preached repentance, and—most peculiarly—built outhouses along the roads.   

"Everyone who is able," John would preach, "needs to make a pilgrimage to the outhouse as needed.    Those who cannot or will not make the trip will find...    well, let's just say, you reap what you sow."

People laughed, but they also listened.  And they remembered.  

Herod Antipas, traveling from Tiberias to Jerusalem, stopped at one of John's outhouses near the Jordan.   He had heard of the young preacher—the one who had called him out at his wedding to Herodias—but had not seen him in years.

---

The outhouse was simple:    a wooden frame, a crescent moon carved into the door, and a basket of clean rags hanging beside it.  Herod stepped inside, grateful for the privacy.  

When he emerged, John was standing there, leaning against a tree, arms crossed.  

Herod froze.  

John smiled faintly. "Tetrarch of Galilee.    I didn't expect to see you here."

Herod's eyes narrowed. "You're the one who baptized me.   The one who said it was unlawful for me to marry Herodias."

John nodded. "I did. And it still is."

Herod laughed—a bitter, hollow sound. "You're audacious, I'll give you that.  Do you know how many men I've had killed for less?"

John met his gaze, unflinching. "I do.  But I'm not here to die today."

Herod studied him for a long moment. "Who are you, really?  You're not just a preacher.    You're not just a builder of outhouses."

John hesitated, then said quietly, "I'm the only one around here named John.  Did you know that in England, 'John' is what they call latrines?   And the men who visit prostitutes?"

Herod raised an eyebrow. "That's...  an unfortunate association."

John shrugged. "My father was mute for the whole time I was in my mother's womb. When I was born, he couldn't speak—so he took a large scroll and wrote on it with an ordinary pen what my name would be.  'John,' he wrote. And then his voice came back."

Herod's breath caught. His mind raced back to the council at Cana, the prophecy of Isaiah, the census Quirinius had destroyed. He remembered the words of Isaiah 8:1: 

> "The Lord said to me, 'Take a large scroll and write on it with an ordinary pen:    Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz. '"

Herod's jaw tightened. "A large scroll.  An ordinary pen. Your father wrote your name, and his voice was restored."

John nodded, puzzled by Herod's sudden intensity. "Yes.   Why?"

Herod didn't answer.   Instead, he whispered, almost to himself:   "Swift is the booty, speedy is the prey."

John frowned. "What?"

Herod looked at him sharply. "You were born under the sign.   7 BCE. The same year as Jesusa Bara Bas.    The same year as Jesus of Nazareth."

John said nothing, but his silence was confirmation enough.  

Herod's jaw tightened. "So you're one of them. One of the prophetic children.   Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz."

John's frown deepened. "I don't know what you're talking about.   I'm just John.   The only John around here."

Herod paced, his mind spinning. He had tried to erase the births under the sign.    He had destroyed the census records.  He had built Tiberias, married Herodias, and fathered Salome—all to claim the prophecy for himself.

But here stood John, alive and unafraid, proclaiming his identity without even knowing it.

"You're dangerous," Herod said finally. "You know that, don't you?"

John smiled faintly. "I know."

Herod turned to leave, then paused. "Why do you build outhouses?"

John's smile widened. "Because everyone needs a place to go.   And because it's easier to preach to people when they're not distracted by their own discomfort."

Herod laughed despite himself. "You're a strange man, John."

"So are you, Herod," John replied. 

---

As Herod rode away, he couldn't shake the encounter from his mind. John was Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz—the swift one, the one who rushes to the spoils. And if John was one of the prophetic children, then so were the others.

Jesus of Nazareth.    Jesusa Bara Bas.  One must be Immanuel.   The other?   Who knew.   He would find out. 

Herod's grip tightened on the reins.    He had tried to control the prophecy, to bend it to his will. But the prophetic children were still alive, still preaching, still building their movements.

And now, one of them had revealed himself—without even knowing it.

---

That night, Herod sat with Herodias in Tiberias, staring into the fire. 

"I saw John the Baptist today," he said quietly. 

Herodias looked up, her eyes sharp.   "And?"

"He told me his birth story. His father was mute until he wrote John's name on a scroll. A large scroll, with an ordinary pen."

Herodias's breath caught. "Isaiah 8."

Herod nodded. "Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz. He doesn't even know.   But I do."

He paused, then added, almost as an afterthought: "And that other one.  Jesus of Nazareth."

Herodias's eyes narrowed.  Herod raised a hand before she could speak. 

"I know," he said.  "You told me to leave him be."

Herodias was silent for a long moment.  Then she said, "He's not our competition, Herod.   But John—John and Bara, really—they may be dangerous...  like bulls in a China store, I think.  What will you do?"

Herod's jaw tightened.  "I don't know yet.  But I can't let him keep preaching.  He's dangerous.    He knows too much—even if he doesn't know he knows it."

Herodias reached over and took his hand. "Then we must be careful. If we move against him too quickly, the people will riot. They love him."

Herod sighed. "I know. But I can't just let him wander around, proclaiming himself as the prophetic child—even if he doesn't realize what he's proclaiming.   It undermines everything we've built."

Herodias smiled faintly. "Then we wait. We watch. And when the time is right, we act."

Herod nodded slowly. "Yes. We wait."

But deep down, he knew that the waiting was almost over.

## Part 7: John's Imprisonment (24–26 CE)

By the year 23 CE, Herod's investigators had discovered Draupi's father Drupa in Sychar.   Herodias pored over the Mahabharata with the scholars.   In Herodias' dreams she saw a step forward that she believed would lead to life, even though it felt wrong in the moment.  The captive should be Drupa—not Draupi.  

She went to Herod and told him, "You must take Drupa for the ransom of Bara Bas.   Not Draupadi."

"Why?" asked Herod.

"You will channel Drona, Drupa's childhood friend.  He was a Brahmin, a spiritual class, but you are Kshatriya, born into a ruling class."

"Okay, but why?"

"Drupa has been reincarnated as Brahmin. He has little physical wealth, but his spiritual wealth is profound. You have already taken his whole kingdom from him by driving his granddaughter into Egypt. You must find a way of restoring his kingdom to him."

"I'm still not quite sure I get it."

"If you restore the Brahmin's spiritual kingdom in full, then you will receive the Kshatriya's physical kingdom in full. This is my dream.   If you do this thing, and do it right, your name shall be forever told, and Drupa shall go to his Hindu resting place, but for all the Romans are concerned, he'll be swallowed by Cronus, like any other departed soul."

---

### Drupa Imprisoned (~23 CE)

Drupa was sitting at Jacob's Well when they came for him. He did not resist. 

Herod's captain said, "You are under arrest.   You will remain in custody until Jesusa Bara Bas returns from Egypt and submits to the council's decree."

Drupa looked up at the sky, where the stars were beginning to appear. "Then I will be in custody for a long time," he said quietly.  "But I will not betray my grandchild."

They took him to Tiberias, where he was held in a small cell near the palace.   Herod visited him occasionally, offering him food, wine, and comfort—Drupa drew a hard line between what was comforting and what was temptation.  He demanded that whatever comfort he be provided be provided to all other prisoners as well.

And so Drupa was granted sufficient freedom to survey the other cells in Tiberias, and their lots improved, while Drupa kept his peace of mind.  "You could be free," Herod said one day.  "All you have to do is tell me where Bara Bas is."

Drupa smiled faintly. "You're not being truthful.   You have the scouts to find her, but not the jurisdiction to extract her.   All I know is she's somewhere in Egypt.   Near Alexandria, but as far as where exactly, you surely know better than I."

Herod sighed. "Then you will remain here until she returns."

---

### Jesus and John Petition for Drupa's Release (~24 CE)

When Jesus of Nazareth and John the Baptist heard that Drupa had been imprisoned, they met and traveled together to Tiberias to petition for his release. 

They found Herod in his palace, surrounded by advisors and guards.   Herodias sat beside him, her eyes sharp and watchful.

Jesus of Nazareth spoke first.   "We have come to ask for the release of Drupa, the grandfather of Jesusa Bara Bas.  He is an old man, and he has done nothing wrong."

Herod leaned back in his chair. "He is harboring a fugitive.   Bara Bas was summoned to appear before the council and submit as a Eunuch.  Instead, she fled to Egypt.   Drupa is complicit."

John the Baptist stepped forward. "Drupa is not complicit.  He is a scholar, a teacher, and a man of peace. Release him, and we will find Bara Bas ourselves."

Herod raised an eyebrow. "You will find Bara Bas?   And bring her here?"

John hesitated. "We will speak with her. We will urge her to return.   But we will not force her."

Herod laughed.   "So you offer me nothing.  Why should I release Drupa?"

Jesus of Nazareth said, "Because it is the right thing to do.  Because mercy is greater than law. Because you are a king, and kings are judged by how they treat the powerless."

Herod smiled.   "People judge kings by how they treat the powerless.   True.   If I were to treat the powerless as my rulers, then where would I be?   Guards.   Seize these two, and put them in my last two empty cells.   They will be treated with the same respect and comforts granted the other powerless."

And so Herod's guards seized Jesus and John and put them into cells.   Then Herod went down to speak to Drupa.  "I am releasing you now, because I have both of Drupa's childhood friends.  This is a better ransom I think, even than her grandfather."

Drupa looked at the open door of the cell.  "I cannot voluntarily accept this arrangement.  Jesus and John are innocent of any crime."

**Herod said**, "Ah, but they stir up rebellion in the people. A spiritual rebellion, even if not a physical one."

Drupa was silent.  

Herod paused, his eyes gleaming with sudden recognition. "Wait. The Mahabharata—Drona took half of Drupada's kingdom.   A worldly kingdom. But here, the roles are reversed. You, Drupa, are the Brahmin—keeper of spiritual authority. I am the Kshatriya—king of worldly power."

He smiled, slow and calculating. "So I will do what Drona did. I will take half your spiritual kingdom.  I will return **Immanuel** to you—Jesus of Nazareth, the one who teaches in your synagogues.   But I will keep **Maher Shalal Hash Baz**—John the Baptist, the one who rushes to the spoils.  For Immanuel is none of my concern, but I desire to see a sign from the other."

Drupa said, "Drona, hmm?  So you've read the Mahabharata."

Herod smiled. "And the Torah."

"You speak of prophecy as if it were a game of chess.   But prophecy is not yours to move.   It belongs to God."

"No, I don't move prophecy.  I move myself to stand where it is aimed.  I aim to stand in the place where the plunder and riches come.   So you and Jesus of Nazareth are free to go.   Now, will you maintain your dignity and walk out, or do you need my guards to toss you out on your head?"

Drupa sighed. "You'll keep your prisoners comfortable?"

Herod said, "Of course."

Drupa said, "Then I will walk out with dignity."

On his way out, he saw two of his grandsons before John's cell. John was asking, "Is Jesus the one who is to come, or is it Barabbas?" The two shook their heads.  "No idea."

He walked out of the palace, free but grieving.  He found some disciples of Jesus, and soon Jesus joined them.  The two Upa Pandavas said, "John asked us if you were the one to come, or to expect another?"

Jesus said, "No idea.   I can only say, I've seen the blind receive sight, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised.   But all I'm doing is preaching the good news to the poor."

The Upa Pandavas nodded solemnly. They returned to John and said, "Jesus of Nazareth is here for all.  But Bara Bas will come for you."

John's eyes filled with tears. He looked up at the narrow window, where a sliver of sky was visible. "Then I will wait," he whispered.  "I will wait for the one who is to come."

### John's Sustained Imprisonment (24–26 CE)

For two years, John remained in Herod's custody. He was given the same comforts as Drupa and the other prisoners.   He was not tortured, but he was not free.   He was held in a cell in Machaerus, a fortress on the eastern shore of the Dead Sea.  

Herod visited him occasionally, sometimes alone, sometimes with Herodias. 

One day, Herod asked, "Do you regret it?   Trading your freedom for Drupa's?"

John looked at him steadily. "I had no say in that. But no.   I do not regret it."

"Why not?" Herod asked.  "You could be out there, baptizing, preaching, gathering followers.  Instead, you're here, alone."

John said, "I am not alone. God is with me.   And I am eager for Bara Bas to come for me."

Herod laughed. "Will He? I don't see Him."

John said, "That's because you're looking in the wrong place."

Herodias, who had been silent, spoke up. "You are a strange man, John. You could curse us.   You could rage against us. But you don't."

John said, "I have already said what I needed to say.   It is unlawful for Herod to marry you.  I have no anger toward you.   I did not write the law.   But your marriage is forbidden in more ways than one.   The duty of the Son of Man is to warn of every sin that might end in death.  Is that my responsibility?  Just in case it is, I warn you."

"Have any died to protect our pride?" Herodias wondered aloud.

"Pride?" Herod laughed. "Of course not. For your honor, my dear, and the honor of Rome.   If you dislike death, we could cut off tongues. Or fingers for those who would slander you in writing."

In that moment, Herodias began plotting to be sure John would live.  

---

### Salome at Age Twelve (26 CE)

By the time Salome was twelve, she was no longer the child who had watched her mother whisper over scrolls.  She was a young woman, trained in rhetoric, philosophy, and the arts of persuasion. 

Herodias sat her down and said:  

"You are no longer a child. You are a woman.  And women must be wise, not just beautiful. They must be strong, not just graceful. They must be cunning, not just obedient."

"Why?" Salome asked.

"Because," Herodias said, "the world will try to take everything from you. Your voice.   Your body.  Your choice.  But if you are wise, strong, and cunning, you can take back what is yours."

"What is mine?" Salome asked.  

Herodias smiled. "Everything."

### Gabriel Contacts the Upa Pandavas (26 CE)

In the year 26 CE, Gabriel—the bodyguard who had fathered Salome—made contact with the five Upa Pandavas:   Prat, Soma, Karman, Nika, and Sena.

He found them in Sychar, where they were helping Drupa tend to the well and the surrounding fields. 

Gabriel said, "I have a message from Herodias. She wants you to know that John the Baptist is still alive, and she is planning to free him."

Prat, the eldest, said, "Why would she do that?   She is Herod's wife."

Gabriel looked away, his voice low. "Because she has come to see John as a prophet. And because Salome—my daughter—has asked for it.   Herodias fears what Herod might do if John continues to speak.  She would rather free him than watch him die."

Soma said, "What does she want from us?"

Gabriel said, "She wants you to write to Jesusa Bara Bas in Alexandria. Tell her that it is time to return.   Tell her that John will be freed.   Bara Bas left in her 12th year.   Bara Bas will come for John in Salome's 12th year.   You should assemble 12 people to meet with John when I free him.   This will complete Herod's part in a prophecy and fully restore Drupa's spiritual kingdom."

Nika said, "And what if Bara Bas does not return?"

Gabriel said, "Then John will remain in prison.   And the prophecy will remain unfulfilled."

Sena said, "We will write to her.   But we cannot force her to return."

Gabriel nodded. "I know. But Herodias believes she will come."

---

### Draupadi Writes to Alexandria (26 CE)

That night, the five Upa Pandavas sat with their mother, Draupadi, and composed a letter to Jesusa Bara Bas. 

The letter read:

> Dear Bara Bas,
>
> We write to you from Sychar, where your grandfather Drupa still tends Jacob's Well. He is free, but he grieves for John, who has been imprisoned in your place.  
>
> Herodias, the wife of Herod, has sent word through Gabriel that she plans to free John. She has asked us to tell you that it is time to return.  
>
> John believes you are the one to come.   Eleven disciples must be gathered:   Your most trusted advisors, and your five older brothers volunteer.   John says he will talk to Jesus about becoming your twelfth disciple, as soon as he gets out.  
>
> We do not know what the future holds.  But we believe that the prophecy cannot be fulfilled without you. 
>
> Please return.   We need you.
>
> — Prat, Soma, Karman, Nika, and Sena

They sent the letter to Alexandria with a trusted messenger, and they waited.
